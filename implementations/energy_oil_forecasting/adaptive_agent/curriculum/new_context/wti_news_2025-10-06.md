West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil prices have experienced a notable downward trend, trading in the low $60s per barrel. After closing at $65.72 per barrel on September 26, 2025, WTI fell sharply to $63.11 on September 29 and continued its slide to trade around $61.28 by October 2. Technical analysts note that WTI has broken below key support levels at $63.20–$63.80, testing a critical support trend between $61.60 and $62.00. A failure to hold above this range could expose the market to further downward pressure toward the $59.50 zone. This bearish momentum is primarily driven by rising global supply expectations and persistent oversupply concerns.

 This follows a September 7 decision by eight alliance members—Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman—to proceed with a 137,000 barrels per day (bpd) production increase for October, defying expectations of a pause. Adding to the bearish sentiment, rumors emerged on September 30 suggesting that OPEC+ might accelerate its production increases, potentially adding up to 500,000 bpd from October through December to regain market share. Meanwhile, the Joint Ministerial Monitoring Committee (JMMC) held its 62nd meeting on October 1, emphasizing the critical importance of strict quota compliance and reviewing updated compensation plans from overproducing members like Russia, Iraq, and Kazakhstan.

Despite the prevailing bearish sentiment, geopolitical supply risks continue to provide a floor for prices. Market participants remain concerned about tightening sanctions on Russia and Iran, which could restrict global supply. Specifically, Russian oil production is facing a significant decline due to international sanctions that limit access to the Western technology and equipment required to maintain output. Additionally, the broader market remains sensitive to ongoing tensions in the Middle East and potential disruptions to transit routes.

The global demand outlook remains a point of divergence among major energy forecasters. In its September report, the International Energy Agency (IEA) projected a modest global oil demand increase of 740,000 bpd for 2025. In contrast, OPEC's September report offered a more optimistic forecast, expecting global demand to grow by 1.3 million bpd in 2025. This demand growth is being met by robust non-OPEC+ supply, which the IEA projects will rise by 1.4 million bpd in 2025, led by record or near-record production in countries like the United States. This combination of rising non-OPEC+ output and the gradual unwinding of OPEC+ cuts has reinforced expectations of a looming supply surplus.
