As of February 20, 2025, West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil futures settled at $72.57 per barrel, edging up slightly after finding a technical floor near its 100-day moving average of $71.43. Throughout February, WTI has traded within a relatively tight $5 range, swinging primarily between $67.50 and $77.50. While prices topped $80 earlier in the year due to cold weather and tighter sanctions, they subsequently retreated following tariff threats from the Trump administration. However, recent sessions have seen a modest three-day winning streak driven by emerging supply anxieties.

OPEC+ is reportedly considering a further delay to its planned production increase, which was scheduled to begin in April 2025. If approved, this would mark the fourth time the alliance has postponed rolling back its voluntary output cuts. In its Monthly Oil Market Report (MOMR) released on February 12, OPEC maintained its robust global oil demand growth forecast for 2025 at 1.4 million barrels per day (mb/d). However, the group's actual supply policy remains highly sensitive to compliance among members and evolving macroeconomic pressures.

Geopolitical risks have intensified, providing upward pressure on prices. A Ukrainian drone attack on a pumping station in Russia has threatened to halt up to 30% of oil exports from a major Kazakh pipeline to the Black Sea. Concurrently, the G7 is considering tightening the price cap on Russian crude exports. Trade policy also remains a dominant market driver; while President Trump paused the implementation of 10% tariffs on Canadian energy imports and 25% on Mexican imports for one month to allow for negotiations, the threat continues to worry refiners. Meanwhile, China's retaliatory 10% tariff on U.S. crude oil imports, which took effect on February 10, has further complicated global trade flows.

The demand outlook presents a mixed picture. The International Energy Agency (IEA), in its February 13 report, projected global oil demand growth to average 1.1 mb/d in 2025, with China marginally remaining the largest source of growth despite a slowing expansion driven almost entirely by its petrochemical sector. Conversely, weak refining margins—with crack spreads down more than 25% year-over-year—and sluggish consumer sentiment in the U.S. and Europe continue to cap price gains. On the inventory front, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) reported a substantial build of 4.63 million barrels in domestic crude stocks for the week ending February 14, exceeding Bloomberg's average analyst estimate of a 3.46-million-barrel build.

Prominent financial institutions have updated their WTI price projections to reflect these shifting dynamics. Standard Chartered, in a report dated February 18, projected WTI to average $79/bbl in Q1 2025, rising to $81/bbl in Q2 and peaking at $90/bbl by Q4. In contrast, BMI (a unit of Fitch Solutions) forecast on February 14 that front-month WTI will average a more modest $73/bbl for the full year. Taking a more bearish stance, Morningstar DBRS released a base-case forecast on February 13 projecting WTI to average $65/bbl in 2025 and drop to $60/bbl in 2026.
