In the final week of January 2025, West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil prices fell to their lowest levels of the year so far. On Wednesday, January 29, 2025, WTI crude fell by $1.15 (or 1.6%) to settle at $72.62 per barrel on the New York Mercantile Exchange (NYMEX), while global benchmark Brent crude finished down at $76.58 per barrel. This downward momentum followed a brief period earlier in the week when WTI hovered around $73.53. The price decline was primarily driven by a larger-than-expected build in U.S. crude stockpiles, which rose by 3.46 million barrels due to a consecutive weekly drop in refinery intake. Additionally, warming winter weather forecasts in the U.S. and Europe eased demand for heating fuels, further depressing prices.

OPEC+ continues to enforce strict production discipline to support the market amid a fragile global economic backdrop. The alliance is currently maintaining its voluntary production cuts of 2.2 million barrels per day (bpd) through the end of March 2025, with plans to potentially phase them out gradually starting in April. This extension of cuts reflects the group's ongoing efforts to balance the market against rising non-OPEC supply and uncertain demand growth.

Geopolitical supply risks are heavily centered on tightening U.S. sanctions. Expansive new U.S. sanctions announced on January 10, 2025, targeted Russian oil producers Gazprom Neft and Surgutneftegaz, alongside more than 160 tankers and insurance providers involved in Russian, Iranian, and Venezuelan oil transport. These measures are actively disrupting logistics; for instance, independent refineries in China's Shandong province face losing up to 1 million bpd of crude supply due to local port bans on sanctioned tankers. Furthermore, market participants are closely watching for a potentially tougher stance on Iranian oil exports from the newly inaugurated Trump administration.

The global demand outlook has softened, highlighted by an unexpected contraction in China's manufacturing activity (PMI) in January, which has cast doubt on the world's largest importer's consumption growth. Warming weather in the U.S. and Europe has also dampened short-term heating fuel demand. Major forecasting agencies, including the IEA, estimate global oil demand growth for 2025 to average around 1.05 million bpd, supported by non-OECD countries, though analysts warn that a fundamental mismatch between robust non-OPEC+ supply growth—led by the U.S., Brazil, Guyana, and Canada—and sluggish demand will likely keep global oil prices under pressure.
