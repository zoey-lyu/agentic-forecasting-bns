WTI crude oil prices started June under pressure but remained highly sensitive to Middle East supply disruptions. At the beginning of the month, WTI was trading at approximately $95.96 per barrel, while Brent hovered around $98.29 before climbing above $101 by June 3. By June 5, WTI was trading in the low-to-mid $90s. In its June Short-Term Energy Outlook (STEO) released on June 9, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) projected Brent to average $105 per barrel during June and July, with a full-year 2026 average of $95 per barrel, before falling to $79 in 2027. Global oil inventories are running critically low due to the ongoing Strait of Hormuz closure. Industry executives like Exxon Mobil's Neil Chapman warned that if inventories continue to deplete, Brent could spike to $150 or $160 a barrel, while Rosenberg Research's Mehmet Beceren warned of a tipping point by the end of June.

On the supply side, OPEC+ approved its fourth consecutive output quota hike since the Hormuz closure, adding approximately 188,000 barrels per day (bpd) for July, bringing cumulative quota increases since April to nearly 600,000 bpd. However, physical constraints have severely limited actual output. OPEC's June Monthly Oil Market Report (MOMR), released on June 11, showed that OPEC+ crude production in May fell by 185,000 bpd month-on-month to 33.13 million bpd—the lowest level since 2000. This decline is a physical consequence of the Strait of Hormuz closure, which has blocked Gulf crude export routes. Saudi Arabia, Iraq, and Kuwait are running a combined 8.5 million bpd below their collective quota targets. Meanwhile, Kazakhstan overproduced by approximately 270,000 bpd above its July quota of 1.608 million bpd, with an accumulated overproduction debt of 3.5 million bpd since January 2024. Additionally, the UAE officially left OPEC effective May 1st.

The primary supply risk remains the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz due to the ongoing conflict involving Iran. Middle Eastern producers have reduced oil output by more than 11 million bpd, contributing to massive global inventory draws that averaged 6.3 million bpd in Q2 and are projected to average 7.6 million bpd in Q3, according to the EIA. Consequently, OECD oil inventories have plummeted to their lowest levels since 2003. While rumors of a potential U.S.-Iran agreement have circulated, a concrete deal to reopen the strait remains elusive, keeping marine insurance rates elevated and shipping lines hesitant to resume transits. Iran's own production collapsed by approximately 28% in May compared to February 2026 levels due to the U.S. blockade.

Major forecasting agencies are sharply divided on the 2026 demand outlook. In its June STEO, the EIA projected that global oil demand will actually decline by 1.1 million bpd in 2026 compared to 2025, citing high fuel prices, reduced fuel availability, and government conservation measures, particularly in Asia. Conversely, OPEC's June MOMR projected that global oil demand will grow by 970,000 bpd in 2026. This is a downward revision from OPEC's May forecast of 1.17 million bpd, driven by a 60,000 bpd cut to Indian demand growth and a projected 40,000 bpd fall in Middle East consumption. For 2027, OPEC raised its demand growth forecast to 1.73 million bpd (up from 1.54 million bpd in May), assuming a strong economic recovery and a normalization of transit routes.
