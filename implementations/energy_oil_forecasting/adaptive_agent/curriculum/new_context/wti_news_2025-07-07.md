WTI crude oil prices are trading in the mid-to-high $66 to $67 range, settling at $67.00 per barrel on July 3, 2025. This follows a highly volatile period where prices experienced their steepest weekly decline since March 2023 during the final week of June, dropping nearly 12% as geopolitical risk premiums evaporated. The sharp retreat brought benchmarks down from their mid-June peaks—when WTI briefly spiked to $77.62 amid intense Middle East tensions—to a low of $65.11 on June 30. However, prices staged a temporary 3% rebound on July 2, with WTI settling at $67.45, before giving back some gains.

The market is closely watching the upcoming OPEC+ meeting scheduled for July 6, 2025, where members will decide on August production levels. The alliance of eight key producers (including Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, and the UAE) previously implemented a 411,000 barrels per day (bpd) production hike for July. They are widely expected to approve another 411,000 bpd increase for August, bringing the two-month total supply addition to over 800,000 bpd. While these supply increases are largely priced in by market participants, analysts note that compliance remains a key focus, with Saudi Arabia actively pushing overproducing members like Iraq and Kazakhstan to adhere to their compensation plans.

Geopolitical supply risks have significantly de-escalated following an Iran-Israel ceasefire and subsequent easing of Middle East tensions, which erased the heavy risk premium that had spiked prices in mid-June. However, residual geopolitical volatility flared on July 2, 2025, when Iran announced it was suspending cooperation with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), briefly reintroducing a sentiment-driven risk premium that drove a 3% single-day price spike. Beyond the Middle East, macroeconomic and trade policies are introducing fresh uncertainty; while a newly finalized U.S.-Vietnam trade deal has boosted broader market sentiment, the upcoming July 9 expiration of a U.S. tariff pause with other major nations presents potential downside risks to global economic growth and future oil demand.

Demand-side concerns are currently capping price gains, highlighted by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) reporting a surprise 3.8 million-barrel build in commercial crude inventories for the week ending June 27, bringing total stockpiles to 419 million barrels. This build defied analyst expectations of a 1.8 million-barrel draw. Furthermore, U.S. gasoline demand fell sharply to 8.6 million bpd from 9.7 million bpd the prior week, raising red flags about weak fuel consumption during the peak summer driving season. Despite these headwinds, supportive macroeconomic indicators—such as strong U.S. employment data (147,000 jobs added in June) and an expansion in China's June Caixin Manufacturing PMI to 50.4—provide a partial demand floor. In terms of forecasts, technical analysts note that if WTI holds above its 100-day moving average, it has a near-term chance of challenging resistance at $66.90. However, broader market analysts warn that if global demand falters under tariff pressures and rising OPEC+ supply, WTI prices could face downward pressure toward the $58 per barrel mark.
