WTI crude oil prices are trading at multi-year highs amid extreme volatility. On March 13, WTI closed at $98.71 per barrel, before climbing to $101.53 on Sunday, March 15, as Middle East hostilities intensified. Following a sharp 5.3% pullback on Monday, March 16, to settle at $93.50, prices rebounded on Tuesday to $95.98 and stabilized near $96.32 on Wednesday. By March 19, WTI front-month futures hovered between $96 and $97 per barrel. A defining feature of the current market is the dramatic blowout of the Brent-WTI spread, which widened to a prompt-dated discount of $12.63 per barrel on March 19—the widest since January 2014. This widening reflects Brent absorbing severe international supply risks while WTI remains relatively insulated by domestic logistics and policy dynamics.

The primary driver of the price surge is the war between the U.S.-Israel coalition and Iran, now entering its third week. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively blocked since February 28, halting approximately 20% of global seaborne oil trade. Supply risks escalated further following weekend airstrikes on Iran's Kharg Island—which handles roughly 90% of Iranian oil exports—and retaliatory drone attacks on the Fujairah oil terminal in the UAE. In response to this historic disruption, the IEA unanimously agreed on March 11 to coordinate a record emergency stock release of 400 million barrels over four months, with the U.S. contributing 172 million barrels from its Strategic Petroleum Reserve.

OPEC+ has paused its planned production increases, citing seasonal demand patterns and heightened market uncertainty. However, actual production has been severely curtailed by the conflict rather than policy. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz and attacks on regional infrastructure have forced Gulf producers to shut in significant volumes as domestic storage tanks fill up. The IEA estimates that Gulf countries have cut total oil production by at least 10 million bpd, with OPEC+ crude supply plunging by 8.1 million bpd in March.

Demand projections have diverged sharply between major agencies. In its March 11 Monthly Oil Market Report, OPEC maintained its 2026 global oil demand growth forecast at a robust 1.38 million bpd (bringing total demand to 106.53 million bpd), offsetting minor Q2 Middle East weakness with upward revisions to Q1 Chinese demand. Conversely, the IEA's March 12 report slashed its 2026 global demand growth forecast by 210,000 bpd to 640,000 bpd, citing a contraction of over 1 million bpd in March and April due to soaring prices and economic fallout. Meanwhile, the U.S. EIA's March Short-Term Energy Outlook boosted its average 2026 WTI spot price forecast by $20 to $73.61 per barrel, projecting a Q2 peak average of $84.56. Analysts at Kpler warned that rumors of a potential U.S. crude export ban are heavily discounting WTI, noting that trapping 4 million bpd of light-sweet shale domestically would quickly exhaust U.S. storage and force production shut-ins.
