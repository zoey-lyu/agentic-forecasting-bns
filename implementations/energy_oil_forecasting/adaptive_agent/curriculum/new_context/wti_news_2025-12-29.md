As of late December 2025, West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil is trading in a tight range near $58 per barrel, settling at $58.35 on Christmas Eve and holding steady in thin holiday trading. Despite a modest mid-week rally driven by geopolitical headlines, WTI remains on track for its largest annual decline since 2020, down approximately 18% to 19% over the course of the year. This downward trend reflects a persistent struggle between immediate geopolitical supply risks and a looming global supply overhang projected for 2026.

Geopolitical tensions are currently providing a floor for prices, highlighted by the Trump administration's aggressive blockade against sanctioned Venezuelan oil tankers. The U.S. Coast Guard's recent boarding and pursuit of vessels off the Venezuelan coast have raised concerns over near-term supply delays. Additionally, market participants are closely monitoring escalating risks in the Black Sea, where Ukrainian forces have targeted Russia's shadow fleet, and renewed friction in the Strait of Hormuz, where Iranian threats to restrict commercial shipping transit have added a fresh risk premium.

On the supply side, the market is bracing for a substantial glut in 2026 as production from both OPEC+ and non-OPEC+ nations remains robust. While OPEC+ previously drove supply reductions—led by sanctions-hit Russia and Venezuela—broader global supply growth is expected to outpace demand. The stark contrast between surging crude supplies and unexpectedly tight product markets has kept refinery margins elevated, though overall physical crude availability remains well-stocked.

The global demand outlook remains muted, with signs of weakening consumption acting as a strong headwind against sustained price rallies. Although the International Energy Agency (IEA) recently upgraded its global oil demand growth forecasts for 2025 and 2026 to 830,000 b/d and 860,000 b/d respectively, the broader market remains highly cautious. Analysts like Dennis Kissler of BOK Financial Securities note that while U.S. blockades delay rather than decrease global supplies, they offer only temporary bullish support. Meanwhile, Priyanka Sachdeva of Phillip Nova highlights that while the physical market is currently adequately supplied, any further escalation in the Strait of Hormuz or additional sanctions on Iranian exports could quickly tighten sentiment and inject fresh risk premiums into the market.
