As of early September 2025, West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil prices are experiencing downward pressure, trading around $63.48 to $63.50 per barrel on September 4, 2025. This represents a stabilization of prices following a sharp decline on September 3, when WTI fell by 2.88% to settle at $63.51 per barrel. The downward trajectory has been building since late August, when WTI dipped to $64.09 per barrel on August 29. Speculative selling pressure has heavily weighed on front-month contracts, with hedge funds and money managers turning increasingly bearish as the market transitions out of the high-demand summer season.

The primary driver of this bearish sentiment is the ongoing unwinding of OPEC+ supply curbs. On August 3, 2025, eight key OPEC+ members (including Saudi Arabia, Russia, the UAE, and Iraq) agreed to implement a production increase of 547,000 barrels per day (bpd) for September 2025. This move represents a phased reversal of the 2.2 million bpd voluntary cuts introduced in late 2023. Ahead of the upcoming OPEC+ ministerial meeting scheduled for September 7, 2025, market participants are closely watching whether the group will pause or proceed with further output hikes. While some analysts expect the group to hold production steady due to falling prices, reports indicate that some members are considering additional increases for October to reclaim global market share.

Despite the bearish price action, geopolitical risks continue to inject volatility and maintain a risk premium. The Russia-Ukraine conflict remains a prominent threat, highlighted by Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil terminals. Additionally, the U.S. recently imposed new sanctions on a shipping network smuggling Iranian oil, raising concerns over tighter future supplies. Trade tensions are also escalating; the U.S. has threatened or imposed tariffs of up to 50% on countries like India to deter them from purchasing Russian crude, complicating global trade flows and prompting major suppliers like Saudi Aramco to halt some sales to Russian-backed Indian refineries.

The demand outlook remains sluggish, characterized by a "tug of war" between rising supply and softening global consumption. The International Energy Agency (IEA) has projected global oil demand growth to slow to roughly 680,000 to 700,000 bpd for 2025, citing weak economic indicators in major non-OECD economies like China, India, and Brazil. In the U.S., the end of the summer driving season following the Labor Day holiday is expected to further reduce domestic consumption, while contracting manufacturing activity continues to weigh on the macroeconomic outlook. Analysts at ANZ Research warn that further OPEC+ supply increases during this lean demand season could worsen a projected winter surplus. Furthermore, Vivek Dhar of the Commonwealth Bank of Australia suggests that OPEC+ is becoming comfortable with lower prices, which could push WTI into the high-$50 to low-$60 range, potentially challenging the economic viability of U.S. shale production.
