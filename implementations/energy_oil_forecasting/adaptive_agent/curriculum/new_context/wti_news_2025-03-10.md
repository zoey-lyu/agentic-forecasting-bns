WTI crude oil prices experienced a sharp selloff during the week, hitting multi-year lows before stabilizing slightly. On Wednesday, March 5, 2025, WTI crude dropped $1.95 (or 2.86%) to settle at $66.31 a barrel, marking its lowest level since May 2023. Brent crude similarly fell below the $70 mark, settling at $69.30. By Thursday, March 6, prices steadied slightly with WTI trading around $66.60 to $66.70 a barrel. This downward pressure was driven by a combination of a larger-than-expected U.S. crude inventory build, OPEC+'s surprise decision to proceed with its planned output increase, and escalating global trade tensions.

In a virtual meeting on Monday, March 3, 2025, eight key OPEC+ members—including Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman—surprised market participants by reaffirming their plan to proceed with a gradual rollback of their 2.2 million barrels per day (bpd) voluntary production cuts starting April 1, 2025. The unwinding will begin with a modest monthly increase of 138,000 bpd in April. While the alliance emphasized that this increase remains flexible and can be paused or reversed depending on market conditions, the decision to add supply to an already soft market heavily weighed on investor sentiment. Additionally, overproducing members agreed to submit updated, front-loaded compensation schedules to the OPEC Secretariat by March 17, 2025, to offset previous quota violations.

Supply dynamics are further complicated by shifting U.S. trade policies and targeted sanctions. On March 4, 2025, broad U.S. tariffs on Canada, Mexico, and China officially took effect. Notably, Canadian energy exports to the U.S. became subject to a 10% tariff on March 6, though potential exemptions for crude oil were being discussed, which provided a minor cushion to prices late in the week. On another front, the U.S. administration revoked Chevron's license to operate in Venezuela, a move that analysts estimate could eventually remove up to 200,000 bpd of crude supply from the global market.

The global demand outlook has weakened due to fears of economic slowdowns triggered by these reciprocal tariffs. Highlighting this risk, JP Morgan analysts warned that a 100-basis-point drop in U.S. GDP growth could reduce global oil demand by 180,000 bpd. On the domestic front, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) reported a substantial crude inventory build of 3.6 million barrels for the week ending February 28, far exceeding the expected 341,000-barrel increase and signaling softer near-term demand. June Goh, a senior oil market analyst at Sparta, noted that import tariffs are ultimately inflationary and will negatively impact consumption patterns, particularly for diesel, which is highly correlated with industrial demand. Meanwhile, Bjarne Schieldrop of SEB AB observed that while crude has entered oversold territory, the recovery momentum remains fragile as the market digests the dual headwinds of rising OPEC+ supply and tariff uncertainties.
