# WTI Market Context — 2025-02-17

WTI crude oil prices experienced significant volatility during the week of February 7 to February 13, 2025, ultimately trending downward. After starting the week with modest gains—rising to $71.90 per barrel on Monday and peaking near $73.32 on Tuesday—WTI suffered a sharp decline of $1.95 on Wednesday, February 12, to settle at $71.37 per barrel. This mid-week drop represented a weekly decline of over 2%. The downward pressure was primarily driven by a combination of rising domestic inventories, hotter-than-expected U.S. inflation data, and shifting geopolitical expectations.

In its Monthly Oil Market Report (MOMR) released on February 12, 2025, OPEC maintained its global oil demand growth forecast for 2025 unchanged at 1.4 million barrels per day (mb/d), with non-OECD nations driving the bulk of this expansion. This came alongside the alliance's previously reaffirmed stance to begin rolling back voluntary production cuts starting in April 2025. However, market participants remain cautious about how OPEC+ will manage compliance and navigate the potential supply surplus as these cuts are gradually unwound.

Geopolitical developments heavily influenced the market's risk premium. Prices fell on news that Donald Trump announced interest from both Russia and Ukraine in initiating peace talks, sparking optimism that sanctions on Russian crude exports could eventually ease and boost global supply. Conversely, trade tensions escalated as the U.S. imposed 25% tariffs on steel and aluminum imports, prompting retaliatory tariffs from China, including a 10% duty on American crude imports. Meanwhile, in the Middle East, Hamas indefinitely delayed a scheduled hostage release, prompting warnings of severe consequences from Trump by the upcoming weekend, which kept a baseline geopolitical risk premium intact.

The demand outlook remains clouded by macroeconomic headwinds and rising supply. The International Energy Agency (IEA), in its February 13 report, projected global oil demand growth to average 1.1 mb/d in 2025, noting that while China remains the largest source of growth, its expansion has slowed significantly and is heavily reliant on the petrochemical sector. On the domestic front, the Energy Information Administration (EIA) reported that U.S. crude inventories surged by 4.1 million barrels for the week ending February 7, far exceeding analyst expectations of a 3-million-barrel build. This inventory build, combined with a hotter-than-expected January CPI print of 0.5% m-o-m, has stoked fears of prolonged high interest rates and weaker energy consumption.

Financial institutions are increasingly projecting a looser market for the latter half of the year. Notably, Citigroup (Citi) issued a bearish forecast, predicting Brent crude will fall to $60–$65 per barrel in the second half of 2025 (which would drag WTI down proportionally). Citi analysts highlighted that non-OPEC+ supply growth, particularly from the Americas, is poised to outpace weak, tariff-impacted global demand, shifting the global balance into a surplus even if OPEC+ extends its cuts. Other major banks, such as Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan, have also aligned their late-2025 forecasts to reflect a softening price environment, with JPMorgan targeting Brent at $69 per barrel by the fourth quarter.
