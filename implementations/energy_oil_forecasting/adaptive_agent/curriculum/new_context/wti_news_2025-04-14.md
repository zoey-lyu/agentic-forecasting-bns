WTI crude oil prices have experienced a severe downward spiral, plummeting to their lowest levels in four years. On April 8, 2025, WTI fell below the critical $60 per barrel threshold, closing at $59.58 after hitting intraday lows near $58.95. By April 10, WTI was trading around $60.07 per barrel. This represents a massive drop of approximately 14% to 15% since the beginning of April. The rapid sell-off has been characterized by analysts as a reaction to a "toxic cocktail" of escalating global trade tensions and an unexpected supply boost from OPEC+.

On April 3, 2025, eight core OPEC+ members—including Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman—held a virtual meeting and surprised the market by accelerating their production timeline. Rather than sticking to the previously scheduled 135,000 barrels per day (bpd) increase for May, the coalition agreed to triple the hike, adding 411,000 bpd to the market next month. While OPEC+ officially attributed the decision to "healthy market fundamentals", the group emphasized that the phased return of their 2.2 million bpd voluntary cuts remains flexible and can be paused or reversed if market conditions deteriorate. Additionally, overproducing members have committed to submitting updated compensation plans to the OPEC Secretariat by April 15.

The primary catalyst for the market's bearish turn is the rapid escalation of a global trade war. Following President Donald Trump's April 2 Executive Order establishing a baseline 10% tariff on all U.S. imports, China retaliated on April 4 by imposing 34% tariffs on U.S. goods. Tensions escalated further on April 8 when the White House confirmed that a 104% tariff on Chinese imports would take effect. On the geopolitical front, President Trump announced plans for direct talks with Iran regarding its nuclear program, though Iran clarified these would be indirect. Meanwhile, U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright warned of tighter sanctions if Iran fails to cooperate, adding a layer of geopolitical supply risk to the Middle East.

The threat of a full-blown trade war has severely dampened the global demand outlook, triggering widespread recession fears. In its Short-Term Energy Outlook (STEO) released on April 10, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) lowered its global oil demand growth expectations through 2026, citing the negative economic impacts of recent trade policies. Prominent analysts have adjusted their forecasts accordingly; Goldman Sachs has significantly lowered its price expectations, projecting WTI to fall to $58 per barrel by December 2025. Furthermore, J.P. Morgan analysts suggest that the U.S. administration is actively aiming to push oil prices down toward $50 per barrel, even if it causes significant disruption to domestic shale producers.
